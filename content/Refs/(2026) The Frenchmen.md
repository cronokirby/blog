Eakin promises an ambitious survey of the core group of French post-modernist thinkers of the 20th century, and mostly delivers. Following in their footsteps, the book does not limit itself to a mere exposition of ideas, but is primarily concerned with the biographical elements.

The book is organized in mostly chronological order, usually with a focus on a particular philosopher, exposing their main ideas as seen through their publications. Some philosophers make a recurring appearance, giving us a sense of the interactions and influences between the thinkers. Eakin fluidly shifts perspective between them, often guided by links between ideas, or particular events. Despite this breaking the chronology, it's never unclear what themes are at stake, what time the events being narrated are happening in, or who the main subject currently is.

The depth of exposition is well-done. This is not as comprehensive as books that would focus exclusively on the subject or a particular philosopher, nor is it as detailed as the works themselves, of course, but there's enough to understand the key developments, the ideas, connections, and dialogue. After reading this, you get a decent idea of what the field looks like, and have a preparation for further reading. The level of detail varies fluidly as well, from high level to particularities. For example, a concrete analysis of a Poe story by Lacan is presented, so that we might better understand the critique forwarded by Derrida.

One underdeveloped aspect is the autobiographical. Towards the start of the book, the framing device is that of the author's own intellectual journey. We start with her in college, her interest in theory, and even her visit to the École Normale, a school where many of the subjects studied. This was very rewarding, and quite clever, but unfortunately drops out quickly, only to return at the end of the book. As is, the autobiographical element feels tacked on. It's a good experiment, but isn't fleshed out enough to really augment the book. Most of the book feels like your standard intellectual or biographical survey.

Another weakness is in the inconsistent treatment of different authors and subjects. This is inevitable for any treatment, especially one like this which tries to mix both biographical elements and intellectual ones. One approach tries to give even weighting to periods of time and their importance, and the other tries to consider ideas and their importance. It's hard to balance these, and so some aspects are underdeveloped. Ultimately, the subject is complicated and rich enough to merit a more prolonged study, so we can't fault the book too much.

Overall, still worth a read, as a great introduction giving a sense of opening and clarification to an otherwise obscure subject.
